SystemEarth and the EUDR: a plot check that keeps getting re-run.

SystemEarth helps companies prove that farm plots behind their goods are not on land cleared for farming after 31 December 2020. This page explains the company, the EU rule, and why checking a plot against satellite layers is a data problem that never finishes.

20 min readLevel: contextEvent sources: docs/problem_statement_reference.txt, docs/sustainability_kickoff_reference.txtPublic facts checked 16 Oct 2026
If you read nothing else
  • The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) says certain goods may only be sold into the EU if they come from plots that were not deforested after 31 December 2020. Plot boundaries must be supplied and checked.
  • Today's application dates, checked 16 October 2026: 30 December 2026 for large and medium operators, 30 June 2027 for micro and small ones.

1 · The company

SystemEarth checks farm plots against map layers.

The challenge slides carry the footer © 2026 SystemEarth Pte Ltd. They describe what the company does in one sentence: for every farm plot, it overlays several geospatial layers to answer EUDR questions. Was the land forested at the cut-off date? Has it been deforested since? Is it legal to produce there?

The layers named on the kickoff slides are:

  • a natural forest baseline: forest presence on 31 December 2020;
  • tree cover loss and deforestation alerts: annual loss, plus near-real-time alerts;
  • protected and restricted-use areas, and land legality layers from national and international datasets;
  • plot geometries, monitored over time: polygons checked against every layer and "re-checked as layers update year after year".

The printed booklet (p. 9) adds the problem: the decision "rarely needs the entire raw value of data", only segments of pixel information, so the challenge is handling vast amounts of it "in a performant and accurate manner".

2 · The rule

The EUDR in plain English: show where it grew, and that it wasn't cleared.

The EU Deforestation Regulation is Regulation (EU) 2023/1115. In one sentence: companies that bring certain goods into the EU market, or export them, must show those goods did not come from land deforested after a fixed date, and were legally produced.

What is covered

Seven commodities, plus products made from them: cattle, wood, cocoa, soy, palm oil, coffee and rubber (so leather, chocolate, tyres, furniture and similar). The Commission's own page lists them, and notes a delegated act adding some palm oil derivatives, soluble coffee and frozen cattle tongues from 30 December 2027. Source: European Commission, environment.ec.europa.eu, checked 16 Oct 2026.

Where it grew: geolocation of production plots

The operator must know the plot of land where each commodity was produced. For plots over 4 hectares (cattle excepted) that means a polygon outlining the boundary. For smaller plots a single latitude and longitude point can be enough. Coordinates need at least six decimal places. Source: Fairtrade guide on EUDR geolocation data, which states "Last update: 16 April 2026"; checked 16 Oct 2026. The supplier plots we were given range from 0.53 to 20.07 ha, and all come as polygons.

"Deforestation-free" and the cut-off

"Deforestation-free" means the goods were not produced on land that was deforested after 31 December 2020. Anything cleared before that date is outside the rule; anything cleared after it is the problem. The rule also needs the production to be legal, which is why the slides list land legality and protected areas too.

What counts as forest

The forest definition we ground our work in, from the JRC product metadata quoted in our layer notes: land over 0.5 ha with trees higher than 5 m and canopy cover over 10% (or trees able to reach those thresholds where they stand), excluding land predominantly in agricultural or urban use. Cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber and soya plantations are not forest. That last line matters for us: a rubber or oil-palm block that is cut and replanted is not automatically "deforestation".

Due diligence

Before the goods are placed on the EU market, the importer (called the operator) must collect information including plot geolocation, assess the risk that the goods are not compliant, reduce that risk if it is more than negligible, and file a due diligence statement in the EU information system. SystemEarth's own site mentions fixing GeoJSON errors "before they cause issues on submission to the EU Information System", which fits that step. The plot check sits inside the risk assessment.

3 · The cut-off

Loss only matters if it is on forest, and after 2020.

Two map layers do two jobs. One says which land was forest at the end of 2020 (we use the EU Joint Research Centre's 2020 forest map). The other says which year each map cell lost its tree cover (we use the Hansen Global Forest Change maps). Put them together and the questions become simple.

Go deeper: two details of the cut-off technical

Year resolution. Hansen records the year of loss, so loss in 2020 cannot be told apart from loss on 31 December 2020. We treat 2021 as the first year after the cut-off.

Loss before 2021 on forest. If the 2020 forest map says a cell is forest, an earlier Hansen loss in that cell does not override it (the forest may have grown back, or the two maps may differ). The 2020 forest map is the baseline.

4 · Why it is a data problem

Every plot, against every layer, every year, again.

One plot check is easy: take the boundary, find the map cells inside it, read the forest and loss values, answer. The hard part is the multiplication.

  • Many plots. A company's suppliers can register millions of boundaries.
  • Several layers. Forest baseline, annual loss, alerts, protected areas, legality.
  • Many years. Loss maps add a new year, and a new release can also revise earlier years.
  • Re-checking. The slides say plot polygons are "re-checked as layers update year after year". A plot that was fine last year has to be asked again.

So there are two different workloads, and Plotline was shaped around telling them apart. A new layer release means every registered plot is checked again against the same, unchanged boundaries. A new plot means one boundary arrives and needs one check. They behave differently in practice: the first can reuse the cells found for each plot last time, the second cannot.

5 · The user

An analyst with a pile of supplier claims, and a sceptical engineer.

Plotline's recommended direction (docs/planning/systemearth-product-direction.md, a recommendation, not an extra company requirement) has two readers in mind.

Primary user

The analyst

A company analyst reconciling a supplier's due-diligence spreadsheet with farm boundaries and mapped observations. The question they bring: which supplier claims need review? They need to see what the supplier declared next to what the maps show, and what evidence is missing.

Second reader

The technical reviewer

Someone at SystemEarth deciding whether a smaller representation can be trusted. Their question: can we answer using less data without changing the evidence? They need the proof: same answer from original and compact data, plus size and speed.

Not a risk scoreThe product keeps four things apart: input or join problems, supplier claim versus mapped observation, disagreement between source layers, and encoding mismatches. An unknown is never shown as "clear".

7 · What we are careful about

Screening evidence is not a legal determination.

Everything Plotline reports is mapped loss on mapped 2020 forest, under a written rule, on two public map products. That is useful evidence for a reviewer. It is not a finding that anyone broke the law.

What we can say

Preservation

The compact layer gives identical plot-query outputs under our documented rule, for every cell and so every possible plot boundary in the region. That measures preservation of the answer, not the accuracy of the maps.

A question for the reviewer

Declarations

Supplier statuses are declarations, not facts and not proven violations. Where they do not line up with the maps, that is a question for the evidence files. We never say "red flag" or "non-compliant".

What we do not cover

Gaps in the rule

The 0.5 ha minimum area, the "able to reach 5 m in place" test, degradation, protected areas (WDPA licence unclear), land legality. Our counts are cells, not hectares.

CarefulDo not say "deforestation found", "hectares deforested", "accurate" or "ground truth". Do not say the supplier is lying: their five "indication of deforestation" records cannot be reproduced from the layer they name, but their evidence files were not supplied. 8 · Open questions

What we would ask SystemEarth next.

These come from docs/lab/company-next-conversation.md, an unsent discussion brief, not a statement from the company. The smallest useful next step it proposes is one short review of one plot and one receipt.

Question 1

What should the reference query reproduce?

Which exact historical layer release, spatial inclusion rule, forest definitions and missing-data rules produced the supplied results? For one plot, our rule sees no mapped loss in 2021–23 among 87 selected cells while its row declares an indication. That is an investigation prompt, not an accusation.

Question 2

What evidence should the reviewer inspect next?

The sample gives opaque reference keys, not files. What do they point to, and what is the review step that follows an unresolved one?

Question 3

Which workload should pick the representation?

One interactive plot, a batch of thousands, repeated fixed-cohort checks, or arbitrary regions after updates? A plain GeoTIFF of our decision codes is 8,532 B against our store's 6,146 B, so the best container depends on the operation.

Also open: allowed use of the sample in a public repo, demo and slides; the final commodity and region; the actual submission and pitch logistics.
